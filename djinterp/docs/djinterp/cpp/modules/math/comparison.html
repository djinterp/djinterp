djinterp math: the framework as proposed, beside similar libraries

A comparison of features, made in chunk 34 (step 19 of 48) at the owner's asking, and brought up to date by each chunk since (chunk 35, step 20, its matrix arithmetic; chunk 36, step 21, linear algebra's functions and the owner's rulings on complex numbers and quaternions; chunk 37, step 22's first part, the C core's complex numbers; chunk 38, its second part, the linear algebra they complete; chunk 39, its third, the C++ face of complex numbers; chunk 40, step 23's first part, complex numbers in the language; chunk 41, the first chunk of its second part, their functions; chunk 42, its second chunk, the five complex functions the C core lacked; chunk 43, its third part, matrices of complex numbers; chunk 44, step 24's first part, the C core's quaternions and their C++ faces; chunk 45, its second part, quaternions in the language; chunk 46, its third, matrices of them, their rotations and dynamic matrices of every element type; chunk 47, a term compiled once; chunk 48, one quaternion class): what djinterp's math framework does or is planned to do, by the roadmap's 51 steps (48 until chunk 36's rulings added 3d to 3f, every step from 22 on three further), beside the libraries a user would weigh it against. It compares features alone. How fast each is, and how accurate its functions are in ulps, are Phase 7's measurements (steps 41 and 42); nothing here is measured.

In the djinterp column, "done" means in the tree as of chunk 48, with the step that did it; "step N" means the roadmap plans it at that step; "later" means the roadmap lists it under "Later, not yet decided"; "none" means no step plans it. In the other columns, "yes" is a documented feature, "part" a narrower one, and "no" one the library does not have. Where a cell is not certain from the library's own documentation it says so.

8. Where djinterp meets, exceeds and falls short

It exceeds the others where its principles go further than any of them:

  • One answer. The same bits at every language level from C99 and C++98 to C++23, and in constant evaluation as at run time. LLVM libc and CORE-MATH give the same bits across platforms at run time, and clang and MSVC are bringing them to constant evaluation; no expression library promises it of a whole language, and no C++ constant-evaluation library (gcem, the standard's constexpr <cmath>) promises correct rounding.
  • Exact by default in the language. 0.1 is 1/10 and 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3 holds, where SymPy reads 0.1 as a float unless asked and math.js needs a configuration; exactness crosses kinds in comparison and survives being put in a matrix (S239).
  • Errors as values. Every failure is a value that passes through, the first kept, with Kleene's logic, where the others raise exceptions or give NaN.
  • Reach. A C core and C++98 faces under the same answers. Of the symbolic libraries only SymEngine has C callers, through a wrapper of its C++; TinyExpr is C, and evaluates doubles alone; none has a C++98 floor.
  • Compile time to run time. reify (step 32) carries a compile-time expression to a run-time term, which none of the others has.

It meets them, once the planned steps are done, in: parsing, printing and evaluating a language of arithmetic, relations, logic and piecewise (ExprTk, muParser, math.js); compiling a term once to be run many times in a type, as muParser's bytecode and math.js's compile() do, though not to machine code, as SymEngine's LLVM back end does (S395); MATLAB's matrix literals, blocks, products, transpose, indexing and its det, inv, solve and eig (math.js); the basic rewrites of math.js's simplify; correctly rounded elementary functions (CORE-MATH, LLVM libc, from step 36); the dense decompositions most users need (Eigen's main ones); and the kinds of big numbers GMP and MPFR have (steps 44 to 49), though not their speed at large sizes.

It falls short, with no step planned, in:

  1. user functions and constants in the language, which every evaluator here has;
  2. special functions: C99's own tgamma, lgamma, erf and erfc are missing from the core (asinh, acosh, atanh, log1p and expm1 came with step 22's complex functions), and Boost.Math's breadth (Bessel, zeta, elliptic, distributions) is far off;
  3. computer algebra past the basics: expand, factor and gcd, symbolic solving, symbolic limits, differential equations and assumptions past step 25's declared domains (S274), where SymPy and GiNaC have two decades of work and SymEngine more than one;
  4. element-wise operators, broadcasting and slices, which math.js and NumPy-style languages have (S246, S249);
  5. sparse matrices and SIMD, which Eigen has;
  6. LaTeX, code generation and language bindings;
  7. multiplication of very large numbers by FFT, which GMP and FLINT have;
  8. maturity: most of the others have a decade or more of users, which no plan replaces.

9. What the comparison suggests

For the owner's consideration, the best additions by what they give, each a decision of his to take or leave:

  1. Done in steps 20 and 21: products of matrices kept in order by simplification (S230's review, S251); matrix arithmetic delegated to the C core's mat.h and vec.h and to linear_algebra's generic matrices (S237's review, S247); and the C core's d_mat_power by squaring, so that a dense power has one answer (S248's review, S262). Matrix variables declared are ruled for step 25, as declared domains (S274).
  2. User functions and constants in expr_context, a small step with a large gain: every evaluator compared has them.
  3. C99's missing functions in the C core, then in the language: the inverse hyperbolic functions, log1p and expm1 are in the C core (step 22, chunk 37, S286) and in the language since chunk 41 (S203, S338), their complex values with them, log1p's and expm1's since chunk 42 (S342, S347); the gamma and error functions are still open, each to have its _c form, so "one answer" covers them too.
  4. Complex numbers as a value kind, so that eig's eigenvalues are whole: ruled by the owner on 2026.10.27 for steps 22 and 23, with quaternions in step 24 (S265 to S278); the C core finds every pair since chunk 38 (S292), the C++ face gives them as math::complex<T> since chunk 39 (S317), a complex number is a value of the language since chunk 40 (S327), with its functions since chunks 41 and 42 (S336, S347), and the language's eig gives every eigenvalue, of complex matrices too, since step 23's third part, chunk 43 (S352), and of matrices of quaternions their standard eigenvalues since step 24's third part, chunk 46 (S389): done.
  5. Polynomial algebra (expand, factor, gcd, a canonical form) beside simplify (S231's review), before step 25's derivative needs a canonical form to simplify into.
  6. Assumptions, the conditions under which simplification holds (S228's review).
